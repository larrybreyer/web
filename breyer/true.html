Notes toward a taproot — on truth

What is true?

Three small pictures, tried out on paper before trusting them to a sentence.

I.

Which number is true?

The usual convention is a habit borrowed from switches: true is 1, false is 0. It works, but it was never argued for — it's just where counting starts. Ask instead which digit truth actually deserves, and the case runs the other way.

There is exactly one way to be right about a thing, and a crowd of ways to be wrong about it. Off by a little, off by a lot, off in the wrong direction entirely — false is a whole landscape. True is a single point in it.

On a line, on a plane, in any coordinate system, there is only one point like that: the origin. Zero isn't a small number among others — it's the one place every axis agrees to meet. Let zero be true. Everything else on the line is simply some particular way of missing it.

one true point at the origin · false scattered at every distance from it

II.

Must it be one or the other?

Common sense hands you a two-row form and calls it complete: a thing is true, or it is false. For a great many everyday claims that's fine — the coin lands, the light is on or off.

But plenty of real tests don't resolve that cleanly. The evidence isn't in yet. The question contradicts its own premise. The thing being asked about doesn't exist in a way that makes true or false apply at all. Forcing a verdict there isn't rigor, it's peer pressure wearing the clothes of logic.

A third row costs nothing and refuses nothing. It just tells the truth about the limits of the test.

III.

Who wins?

Most disagreements get read through a single lens: someone wins, someone loses. It feels like the whole space of outcomes, because it's the story we're handed before we even sit down — but it's only the diagonal of a larger grid.

Lay out both outcomes for both people and two more boxes appear, quietly, at the corners: we both come out ahead, or we both come out worse. Neither fits the contest narrative, and both happen constantly — a negotiation, a hard conversation, a disagreement between people who actually like each other.
